## Institutional Background

China's export-processing sector grew rapidly after the country opened to foreign investment and adopted preferential customs regimes. The General Administration of Customs distinguishes two main processing-trade arrangements. Under pure assembly (lai-liao jiagong), a foreign client supplies intermediate inputs to a Chinese factory, retains ownership of those inputs, pays a processing fee, and receives the finished goods for export. Under import-and-assembly (jin-liao jiagong), the Chinese factory imports inputs on its own account, owns them during processing, and exports the finished product. Factories may be Chinese-owned or foreign-invested. Customs records report both the ownership of the processing factory and the party that controls input purchases, yielding four possible ownership-control combinations [E2; E3; E4].

## What Changed

The variation used by Feenstra and Hanson is not a policy shock but a cross-sectional pattern: the share of processing exports falling into each of the four ownership-control regimes differs systematically across products, destinations, cities, economic zones, and regions. The most common pattern in the data is foreign factory ownership combined with Chinese control over input purchases, especially in the southern coastal provinces where export markets are thickest [E2].

## Implementation and Assignment

Assignment to a regime is a firm-level decision rather than a random or administrative assignment. Firms choose ownership and control structures in response to contracting costs, the specificity of human-capital investments, value-added in processing, bargaining weights, and the quality of local contract enforcement. Chinese customs regulations define the menu of available regimes and record the chosen regime for each transaction. The empirical work uses these records to estimate the parameters of a Grossman-Hart-Moore property-rights model [E2].

## Why This Creates Empirical Variation

Because different products, destinations, and locations generate different contracting environments, the observed frequency of each ownership-control regime varies across city-product-destination cells. This cross-sectional distribution can be matched to a structural model to recover parameters such as the specificity of relationship-specific investments, the probability of contract enforcement, and the bargaining weight of the multinational firm. The institutional detail of Chinese customs regimes provides an unusually rich setting for this exercise [E1; E2].

## Identification Risks

The main risks are (i) self-selection into organizational forms on unobservables; (ii) measurement error in ownership and control indicators; (iii) omitted determinants of organizational choice such as taxes, industrial policy, and transfer pricing; (iv) reliance on cross-sectional variation rather than a natural experiment; and (v) limited external validity beyond Chinese processing trade in 1997-2002 [E2].

## Data Requirements

The canonical design requires disaggregated Chinese customs processing-trade data that report, for each transaction or cell, the year, city, economic zone, product, destination, export value, processing regime, factory ownership, and input-control party. Product and regional characteristics are used to proxy for value-added and contracting costs [E2].
