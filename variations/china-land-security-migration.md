## Institutional Background

Under China's Household Responsibility System, agricultural land is owned by village collectives but use rights are contracted to individual households for fixed periods (initially 15 years, then extended to 30 years). However, village authorities have periodically reallocated land among households to adjust for demographic changes — for example, giving land to newly formed households and taking land from households that have lost members. These administrative reallocations create insecurity: a household that leaves agriculture to work in the city risks losing its land in the next reallocation. Despite central government efforts to reduce reallocations through the 2003 Rural Land Contracting Law and subsequent land certification programs, the frequency and predictability of reallocations vary substantially across provinces and villages. [E1]

## What Changed

The key feature is not a single policy change but persistent cross-province variation in the practice of administrative land reallocations. Households in provinces with frequent reallocations face higher land insecurity, which reduces their willingness to migrate to cities (since migration increases the risk of losing land). The paper documents that land insecurity raises the share of rural households operating farms by over 40 percentage points and depresses agricultural productivity by more than 20%. These effects are comparable in magnitude to all other labor-mobility frictions (such as the hukou system) combined. [E1]

## Implementation and Assignment

The empirical strategy exploits variation in land reallocation practices across Chinese provinces and over time. The data come from multiple waves of the China Household Income Project (CHIP) survey and the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), which record whether households have experienced land reallocations and document their migration and farm operation status. The paper constructs a measure of land insecurity from the empirical distribution of land reallocations in each province. The identifying assumption is that province-level land reallocation practices are not correlated with unobserved determinants of migration, conditional on household characteristics and provincial economic conditions. [E1]

## Why This Creates Empirical Variation

Land reallocation practices vary substantially across Chinese provinces — from near-zero reallocation probability in some provinces to over 50% in others — and this variation has persisted over time. The sources of this variation include differences in provincial land policies, the strength of village collective organizations, local interpretation of the land contracting laws, and historical practices. Crucially, these practices were established through administrative and political processes that are plausibly exogenous to individual household migration decisions. The rich household panel data allow the paper to control for household characteristics, provincial economic conditions, and time effects, isolating the effect of land insecurity on migration and labor allocation. [E1; analytical inference]

## Identification Risks

The main threat is that provinces with higher land reallocation frequency may differ on other dimensions that independently affect migration — such as economic development, proximity to urban centers, the quality of social insurance, or the tightness of the hukou system. The paper addresses this through extensive controls and by exploiting within-province changes in land security over time (such as the rollout of land certification). A second concern is measurement: land insecurity is inferred from the historical frequency of reallocations rather than directly observed, and this may be a noisy proxy for the actual risk perceived by households. [E1; analytical inference]

## Data Requirements

The analysis requires household-level panel data covering migration status, landholding, land reallocation experience, farm operation decisions, and demographic characteristics. The CHIP and CHNS surveys provide this data for multiple waves between 2002 and 2018. Province-level data on land policies, land certification programs, and the legal framework for land reallocations are used to construct instruments and to validate the land security measures. The key challenge is constructing a consistent measure of land insecurity that captures the relevant dimension of property rights uncertainty across different provinces and time periods. [E1]

## Evidence Notes

E1 is the published QJE article. The paper makes three main contributions: (1) it documents a large causal effect of land insecurity on labor allocation — households in provinces with high reallocation risk are substantially less likely to migrate and more likely to continue operating farms; (2) it uses a quantitative spatial equilibrium model to show that land insecurity reduces agricultural productivity by over 20% and depresses rural welfare; and (3) it shows that the effects of land insecurity are comparable in magnitude to all other labor-mobility frictions together, implying that land reform could release a massive amount of labor for more productive non-agricultural employment.
